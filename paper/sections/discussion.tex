\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the farm test measures.} Every model we trained, including the two that earlier work recommended,
reaches 98 to 99\% on the random farm test and loses more than 20 points on photographs from a second collection of
the same group. The original authors did not expect distribution shift to matter~\cite{machuve2022}. Our results show
that it matters more than the choice of network. We cannot say from these data which difference between the
collections causes the drop. The lab-confirmed photographs differ in label source, in when and how they were taken,
and possibly in the farms they came from. Two observations narrow it down. First, exact duplicates are not the cause,
because removing them leaves the farm score unchanged. Second, the errors are mostly missed detections and calls of
healthy or salmonella-like, not swaps between the diseases. A change in how the droppings were photographed is
consistent with this. Healthy droppings in the farm collection fill a median of 2.6\% of the photograph, against 12 to
22\% for the other classes, so photograph framing could act as a shortcut~\cite{geirhos2020}. We have not tested that
directly. Bodempudi et al.\ also found a drop across regions for a binary \emph{Salmonella} task~\cite{bodempudi2026}.
Together the two results suggest that published accuracies on this task should be read as within-collection
accuracies.

\paragraph{Why use a detector.} On photographs our detector is not more accurate than a whole-image classifier. Its
value is that it says where the dropping is. That is what makes a fixed pen camera usable, because a dropping covers
at most about 0.1\% of a pen frame, and it is also what lets the explanation step measure the colour of the dropping
itself rather than the litter around it. The pen-scale fine-tune shows that the detector can learn the size and
setting of a pen camera from public video and pasted droppings alone, without a single labelled pen dropping. It
lost 0.02 mAP50 on close-ups in the process.

\paragraph{Why a microkernel.} A monitor in a barn runs unattended, and a crash in a camera driver or a model runtime
should cost a few frames, not the device. On QNX each stage of our pipeline is an ordinary process, and the
supervisor brought any killed stage back in 20 to 33\,ms while the others kept running. The same structure is
possible on Linux with process supervision, but on QNX the drivers, the file system and the network stack are also
user processes that can fail and restart the same way~\cite{qnx8sysarch}. We did not compare fault recovery against
Linux on the same board, so we do not claim QNX recovers faster. Running the model on QNX also needed one fix in the
platform's packages. The Python binding of ONNX Runtime in the QNX open-source repository does not load, because its
build script treats QNX as Windows and looks for the wrong extension module. We worked around it with a 111-line C
bridge to the runtime's C library and have prepared a patch for the package recipe.

\paragraph{Limitations.} All photographs come from one region of Tanzania and one research group. The farm labels
describe appearance and farm, and only the second collection was confirmed by PCR, so we treat the classes as
screening categories, not diagnoses. The farm split is random by photograph because the release carries no farm or
visit information. The pen test and the test clip use real droppings pasted onto real barn frames, so they measure
whether a pen-sized dropping can be found on unseen litter, not field accuracy on naturally occurring droppings. We
have no labelled droppings from a real pen camera. The detector was trained once. The baselines were trained with
three seeds each but with one set of hyperparameters, which we did not tune. Finally, the edge timings are for
inference only and do not include image decoding, post-processing or power draw.

\paragraph{Future work.} The most useful next step is labelled droppings from fixed pen cameras on more than one
farm, split by farm. Short of that, the farm collection could be re-split with near-duplicate removal before
splitting, and the size shortcut could be tested by cropping every class to the same framing. The box score needs
calibration before it is shown to a farmer, and the 0.25 threshold should be chosen from a risk-coverage curve on
validation data rather than fixed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Classifiers of poultry droppings that score 98 to 99\% on a random split of the Tanzanian farm photographs score
about 75\% on lab-confirmed photographs from a second collection, and this holds for every architecture we tried,
including our detector. We therefore recommend that future work on this task report a second collection alongside the
farm test. A detector cannot fix this drop, but it is what makes a fixed pen camera usable. Fine-tuned on public barn
video with pasted droppings, our YOLO11n detector finds 80\% of pen-sized droppings on unseen cameras with 93\%
precision. The full pipeline runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 under QNX, where any failed stage restarts in tens of
milliseconds while the rest keeps running.
