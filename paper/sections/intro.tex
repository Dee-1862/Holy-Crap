\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Coccidiosis alone was estimated to cost the global chicken industry about \pounds10.4 billion at 2016
prices~\cite{blake2020}. Coccidiosis, salmonellosis and Newcastle disease change the colour and consistency of
droppings, and farmers already look at droppings to judge flock health. This has made dropping photographs a popular
target for deep learning. On the public Tanzanian collection of Machuve et al.~\cite{machuve2022,machuve2021farm} and
what appear to be copies of it, convolutional classifiers report 97.8 to 99.3\%
accuracy~\cite{machuve2022,dhungana2025,degu2023,reyad2024,huda2024}, and several groups have proposed phone, web or
edge systems around them~\cite{degu2023,sakib2026,dhungana2025}.

These accuracies come from random splits of a single collection. A random split by photograph can place
near-identical pictures on both sides, as documented for standard benchmarks~\cite{barz2020}, and leakage of this
kind has inflated results in many fields~\cite{kapoor2023}. The collection has a second property that matters more.
Its healthy and coccidiosis photographs were taken on farms, while its salmonella and Newcastle photographs were taken
from experimentally inoculated birds~\cite{machuve2021farm}, so the setting of a photograph can stand in for its
class. The original authors wrote that they did not expect their model to be affected by distribution
shift~\cite{machuve2022}. Only one study has tested the four-class task on a second collection, a local-farm set
whose labelling is not described in its abstract~\cite{sakib2026}, and none on labels from laboratory tests. The same
group released a second collection, taken on farms and labelled from PCR results~\cite{machuve2021pcr}, which offers
that test. A second gap is the camera. Published systems classify close-up photographs taken by a person. A camera
fixed above a pen sees each dropping as a small object among birds, feeders and litter, and nobody is there to
restart the device when software fails.

We address both gaps with one detector and one honest protocol. Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A benchmark of the classifiers earlier work used (MobileNetV2, EfficientNet-B0), retrained on the same split as
  our YOLO11n detector and tested on the same 679 farm photographs and on 1,255 PCR-labelled photographs that no model
  saw. Every model scores 98.5 to 99.6\% on the farm test, in line with published numbers, and 74.6 to 78.2\% on the
  PCR-labelled photographs, a drop of 21 to 25 points for every model. Our detector does not differ significantly from
  either classifier on the second collection. To our knowledge this is the first test of the four-class task on
  PCR-labelled photographs with the split, the models and every per-photograph prediction released.
\item An audit of both collections. In the farm collection, 49 of the 679 test photographs (7.2\%) are exact copies of
  training photographs, but removing them changes the farm accuracy by at most 0.1 points. In the PCR collection, 19
  pairs of byte-identical files are filed under two different classes. We also show that the detector's box score is
  not a calibrated probability.
\item A pen-scale fine-tune trained with real droppings pasted onto barn video frames, split by camera. On cameras it
  never saw, recall rises from 0.29 to 0.80 and precision from 0.11 to 0.93, at a cost of 0.02 mAP50 on close-ups.
\item A pipeline running on a Raspberry Pi 5 under the QNX microkernel, with our detector and pen model timed on the
  device. Each stage is a separate process. When we killed a stage with \texttt{SIGKILL}, it was restarted in 20 to
  33\,ms while the others kept running. To our knowledge no peer-reviewed work has run poultry health vision on a
  microkernel real-time operating system.
\end{enumerate}

We release the code, the per-photograph predictions, the duplicate hashes and every figure with the table behind it.
