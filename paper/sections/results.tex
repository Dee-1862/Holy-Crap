\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{All models drop on lab-confirmed photographs}
\label{sec:res-bench}

Table~\ref{tab:bench} and Figure~\ref{fig:bench} compare every model on the two test sets. On the farm test all of
them are in the range earlier work reports. MobileNetV2 calls 99.6\% of the 679 photographs correctly (95\% CI 98.7 to
99.8\%), EfficientNet-B0 99.0\% (97.9 to 99.5\%), and our detector 98.5\% (97.3 to 99.2\%) when it always gives its
best guess. On the 1,255 lab-confirmed photographs every model loses more than 20 points. MobileNetV2 falls to 74.6\%
(72.1 to 76.9\%), EfficientNet-B0 to 78.2\% (75.9 to 80.4\%) and the detector to 76.3\% (73.9 to 78.6\%). For each
model the intervals of the two test sets are far apart, so the drop is not sampling noise.

The drop is 25.0 points for MobileNetV2, 20.8 for EfficientNet-B0 and 22.2 for the detector. Our detector does not
classify better than the classifiers earlier work used. A paired exact McNemar test on the 1,255 lab-confirmed
photographs finds no difference between the detector and MobileNetV2 ($p=0.20$) or EfficientNet-B0 ($p=0.16$), while
EfficientNet-B0 is ahead of MobileNetV2 ($p=0.002$). Removing the 38 files that the release places in two classes
raises every model by about one point (MobileNetV2 75.6\%, EfficientNet-B0 79.5\%, detector 77.3\%), so the conflicting
labels do not explain the drop. The models fail on different classes. The classifiers keep far more Newcastle-like photographs (recall 0.70 for MobileNetV2 and 0.88 for
EfficientNet-B0, against 0.53 for the detector), while the detector keeps more healthy ones (0.81 against 0.66 and
0.68). The per-class differences are large, but the differences in overall accuracy are small next to the drop
that every model shows. Under this protocol the published
accuracies of 97.8 to 99.3\% describe photographs like the training photographs, and the architecture matters less than the test set.

\subsection{Where the errors go}
\label{sec:res-errors}

Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the detector's calls on both sets. On the farm test the diagonal holds 95 to 100\% of
every class. On the lab-confirmed photographs most errors are not confusions between diseases. They are photographs
with no detection at all (20\% of healthy and 27\% of Newcastle-like photographs) and photographs called healthy or
salmonella-like by mistake. Newcastle-like recall falls the most, from 94.6\% (35 of 37) to 41.9\% (78 of 186). It has 376 farm photographs, against over 2,000 for each other class (Figure~\ref{fig:recall}).

\subsection{Duplicates do not explain the farm score}
\label{sec:res-dup}

Forty-nine of the 679 farm test photographs (7.2\%) have a training photograph with an identical 256-bit difference
hash (Figure~\ref{fig:dup}). Each pair has the same dimensions and class, and the pair we inspected by eye is the same
image saved under two file names. Every other test photograph differs from its nearest training photograph in at
least 40 bits. The validation split is affected too: 53 validation photographs copy a training photograph, and
validation chose the kept epoch. The detector calls all 49 test copies correctly. Without them its farm accuracy is
97.8\% (95\% CI 96.3 to 98.7\%) at the 0.25 threshold, against 97.9\% with them, and the other rows of
Table~\ref{tab:bench} change by at most 0.1 points (detector always answering 98.4\%, MobileNetV2 99.5\%,
EfficientNet-B0 99.1\%). The copies are a flaw in the split, but they are not why the farm test overstates
performance on new photographs.

\subsection{The box score ranks well but is not a probability}
\label{sec:res-cal}

If the detector only answers when its best box clears a threshold, its answers become more reliable
(Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}). On the lab-confirmed photographs, raising the threshold from 0.25 to 0.80 raises the
accuracy of the answers from 78.8\% to 87.4\%, but the detector then answers only 27.8\% of photographs. Its scores
are also under-confident on both sets (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}). The expected calibration error is 0.177 on the
farm test and 0.172 on the lab-confirmed photographs, and in both cases the photograph is right more often than the
score says. The score should be calibrated, for example by temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017} on the validation split, before a farmer sees it as a percentage.

\subsection{What the lab-confirmed droppings look like}
\label{sec:grades-results}

The three raters agreed well on the grade, with Krippendorff's alpha 0.76 over the 1,255 photographs and pairwise
weighted kappa from 0.71 to 0.84. Agreement on single variables was lower, from 0.56 for colour and 0.57 for
undigested feed to 0.75 for the urate cap and 0.74 for blood. Each rater agreed with itself on the 126 repeats with
weighted kappa 0.71 to 0.86, but gave the identical grade only 37 to 47\% of the time, so a single grade is
precise to about one point. Nine photographs showed no dropping that could be judged, 331 were only partly
visible, and on 234 the raters' grades spread by three points or more. The grades, every rating with its rater's
note and these agreement figures are released with the paper.

The grade follows the PCR class (Table~\ref{tab:grades}). Newcastle disease and coccidiosis droppings look
abnormal, with mean grades 4.6 and 5.6, while salmonella droppings are close to healthy ones, 8.0 against 8.6,
and nearly half of them look normal. The raters scored salmonella droppings as looser (mean form penalty 0.61
against 0.28) and with more undigested feed (0.44 against 0.09).

We expected the models to fail most on infected birds whose droppings look normal. They do not. Over the 899
PCR-positive photographs, the detector is right on 83\% of the 190 that look normal and on 73\% of the 709 that
do not, and MobileNetV2 on 87\% and 76\%. The exception is coccidiosis, where both models are right on only 6 of
the 26 normal looking photographs. The high score on normal looking positives comes from salmonella, which both
models call correctly 93 to 98\% of the time even when the raters saw nothing abnormal. Part of that is a lean
towards salmonella, since MobileNetV2 also calls 72 of the 215 normal looking healthy photographs salmonella
(33\%), and the detector 32 (15\%). Telling a normal looking salmonella dropping from a healthy one by eye is
hard, so cues other than the dropping's clinical signs, such as the surface, lighting or the camera used for one
class, may be helping. These grades cannot show which.

\subsection{Pen-scale detection}
\label{sec:res-pen}

The close-up detector does not work at pen scale (Figure~\ref{fig:pen}). On the held-out pen cameras it finds 29\% of
droppings, only 11\% of its boxes are droppings, and it places 3.55 boxes on an average empty pen tile at the 0.25
threshold. After pen-scale fine-tuning, recall is 0.80 and precision 0.93 on cameras the model never saw, and false
boxes on empty tiles fall to 0.05 per tile. The close-up mAP50 changes from 0.873 to 0.853. Class accuracy at pen
size remains weaker than detection (four-class mAP50 0.65). The pen model should therefore find a dropping, and a
full-resolution patch or a close-up photograph should be judged by the close-up model.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/11_pen_scale_before_after.pdf}
\caption{Pen-scale test on held-out cameras before (close-up model) and after fine-tuning. The pen test uses real
droppings pasted onto real litter.}
\label{fig:pen}
\end{figure}

Whole-photograph classifiers cannot be tested this way. They return one label for an image, and in a pen frame a
dropping covers at most about 0.1\% of the image. Locating the dropping is what makes the pen camera usable, and it is
the main reason to use a detector in this setting rather than a better classifier.

\subsection{The deployed pipeline}
\label{sec:res-qnx}

We also ran the pen model over a three-minute clip from the held-out PIO camera, into which ten test droppings were
pasted at known times. Read at two frames a second with a short persistence rule, it flagged seven, five of them
within two seconds of their appearance, and raised no false flags (Figure~\ref{fig:live}). This run was made on a
laptop, and like the pen test it uses pasted droppings. On the Pi, the deployed pipeline proposes candidates with a
still-spot finder and judges a full-resolution patch of each with the close-up detector. On the Raspberry Pi
5 the detector takes 168\,ms at 320 pixels and 618\,ms at 640 pixels with four threads, and the pen model 608\,ms at
640 pixels. In the deployed pipeline the detector runs only on the candidate patches the spot finder flags, not on every frame.
We did not time the baseline classifiers on the Pi, and we have not measured the detection accuracy of the deployed
pipeline on the Pi. Native message passing between two sections takes 95\,$\mu$s for a 57.6\,KB frame and reply. When
we killed a section with \texttt{SIGKILL}, the supervisor started a new copy of it within 20 to 33\,ms. This is the time from
the supervisor noticing the exit to the new process being launched. The supervisor checks its children every 100\,ms,
so a stage can be down for up to about 130\,ms in total, plus the time the new process needs to start serving. We ran these kills by hand on each section during
development and did not keep a count per section. Killing the spot finder cost the camera one frame and the other
sections none. These kills test our process structure and supervisor. We did not kill a QNX driver or service, such as the camera service or network stack.
